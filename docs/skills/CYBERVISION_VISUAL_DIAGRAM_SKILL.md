# CyberVision / SyberVision Visual Diagram Skill

## 1. Purpose Of This Skill

This skill teaches how to create professional visual network diagrams for ClientSurge Systems. Use it to turn launch work, automation systems, funnels, sales workflows, authority assets, QA gates, dashboards, and operating plans into clean black-and-white visual network plans that humans and AI workers can execute from.

The diagrams are operating maps. They must clarify sequence, dependencies, critical path, float, proof requirements, and current position without decoration or fake certainty.

## 2. When To Use This Skill

Use this skill whenever asked to:

- Create a diagram
- Render a diagram
- Audit a diagram
- Revise a diagram
- Build a visual network plan
- Create a process map
- Create a launch map
- Create an automation diagram
- Create a sales/funnel/SEO/marketing/authority/QA diagram
- Convert a checklist into a visual plan

## 3. ClientSurge Business Context

ClientSurge Systems is an Automated Lead Acquisition System for businesses.

It is not a normal web design agency.

The long-term vision is to become:

"the Amazon of AI automation services for business."

The website is the front-end capture layer. The automations are the product. The AI voice agent is the premium response layer. The funnels are the acquisition engines. The authority assets are the trust engine. The dashboard is the operating brain. The diagrams are the operating maps.

CyberVision / SyberVision-style diagrams for ClientSurge must be:

- Black-and-white
- Printable
- Clean
- Professional
- Operational
- Same-size boxes
- Straight arrows only
- No curved arrows
- White box interiors
- Numbered steps
- Top-left number circles or markers
- Clear objective
- Clear START box
- Clear critical path
- Clear float
- Minimal clutter
- Visually similar to SyberVision 2000 network planning diagrams
- Useful for both humans and AI workers

## 4. Non-Negotiable Diagram Rules

- Use straight arrows only.
- Do not use curved arrows.
- Use same-size containers wherever possible.
- Use white interiors.
- Use black outlines.
- Use printable black-and-white styling.
- Add top-left numbering to every process box.
- Add an objective line near the top.
- Include a clear START box.
- Show the critical path.
- Show float.
- Do not leave unfinished lines.
- Do not push arrows deeply into boxes.
- Avoid clutter.
- Do not create fake dependencies.
- Do not claim fake completion.

## 5. The 121-Point CyberVision Diagram Standard

### Foundational Purpose

1. Every diagram must have a clear business objective.
Why it matters: Without an objective, the diagram becomes decoration.
Application: Put "Objective:" near the top.

2. Every diagram must represent a real process, not a vague idea.
Why it matters: A process can be executed; an idea cannot.
Application: Use action-based steps.

3. Every diagram must help someone make a decision or complete work.
Why it matters: Operational diagrams must reduce confusion.
Application: Make the next action obvious.

4. Every diagram must show sequence.
Why it matters: Processes require order.
Application: Use left-to-right flow.

5. Every diagram must distinguish main path from support branches.
Why it matters: Not all tasks are equally critical.
Application: Keep the critical path visually central.

6. Every diagram must avoid pretending optional work is critical.
Why it matters: False critical paths create bad priorities.
Application: Place optional/support tasks above or below the main line.

7. Every diagram must be printable in black and white.
Why it matters: The user wants physical printouts.
Application: Do not rely on color.

8. Every diagram must work without animation.
Why it matters: It should be understandable on paper.
Application: All logic must be visible statically.

9. Every diagram must be readable from a distance.
Why it matters: Wall diagrams must be scanned quickly.
Application: Use large enough text and clean spacing.

10. Every diagram must be useful to both humans and AI workers.
Why it matters: The diagrams will guide AI execution.
Application: Use precise labels and task references.

### Structural Layout

11. Use a left-to-right flow by default.
Why it matters: It mirrors execution progression.
Application: START on the left, completion on the right.

12. Use straight arrows only.
Why it matters: Curved arrows create clutter and confusion.
Application: No curved connectors unless explicitly requested.

13. Avoid diagonal arrows unless absolutely necessary.
Why it matters: Diagonal lines reduce readability.
Application: Use horizontal and vertical segments.

14. Avoid crossing arrows.
Why it matters: Crossings create visual ambiguity.
Application: Reposition branches before allowing crossings.

15. Keep boxes aligned on a clear grid.
Why it matters: Alignment creates professionalism.
Application: Use consistent x/y positioning.

16. Use consistent spacing between boxes.
Why it matters: Uneven spacing looks amateur.
Application: Measure gaps visually or programmatically.

17. Keep containers the same size whenever possible.
Why it matters: The user explicitly prefers same-size boxes.
Application: Standardize width and height.

18. Use white interiors for containers.
Why it matters: Matches the SyberVision style and improves printability.
Application: White fill, black outline.

19. Use black outlines.
Why it matters: Black outlines print clearly.
Application: Use consistent stroke weight.

20. Avoid gradients, shadows, and decorative fills.
Why it matters: They weaken the operational style.
Application: Keep diagrams clean and functional.

21. Use a large outer border around the diagram.
Why it matters: It frames the visual as a formal plan.
Application: Draw a rectangle around the main working area.

22. Place the title at the top center.
Why it matters: The diagram must be instantly identifiable.
Application: Use uppercase or strong title casing.

23. Include the diagram number near the top.
Why it matters: Manuals need references.
Application: Example: DIAGRAM 0.1

24. Include the diagram type.
Why it matters: Clarifies whether it is a master plan, sub-process, gate, or system map.
Application: Example: VISUAL NETWORK PLAN

25. Include an objective line under the title.
Why it matters: Prevents misinterpretation.
Application: Objective: Launch ClientSurge with measurable lead acquisition infrastructure.

26. Include current position when relevant.
Why it matters: Shows where execution currently stands.
Application: CURRENT POSITION: Mission Control Planning

27. Include critical path notes.
Why it matters: Highlights what must be done first.
Application: CRITICAL PATH: Mission Control -> Leads -> Funnels -> Analytics -> Launch

28. Include float notes when relevant.
Why it matters: Shows work that can move without delaying launch.
Application: FLOAT: Social content, optional blog posts, secondary visuals.

29. Use numbered boxes.
Why it matters: Numbers allow references in manuals and tasks.
Application: Use 1, 3, 5, 7 or sequential values.

30. Number placement should be consistent.
Why it matters: Inconsistent numbering looks sloppy.
Application: Top-left circle or marker inside each box.

### Box Design

31. Every box should represent one task, system, gate, or deliverable.
Why it matters: Mixed boxes create confusion.
Application: Do not combine unrelated tasks.

32. Box titles should be short.
Why it matters: Long text reduces readability.
Application: Use 2-4 words when possible.

33. Use supporting subtitle text sparingly.
Why it matters: Too much text clutters the diagram.
Application: Add a short phrase below the title only when needed.

34. Use action nouns or system names.
Why it matters: The label should communicate what the box does.
Application: "Lead Database" or "Verify Tracking."

35. Avoid vague labels.
Why it matters: "Improve Website" is not specific.
Application: Use "Audit Homepage CTA" or "Install GA4 Events."

36. Each box should have a unique meaning.
Why it matters: Duplicate boxes confuse the reader.
Application: Do not repeat "Marketing" without differentiation.

37. Use consistent capitalization.
Why it matters: Visual consistency matters.
Application: Use uppercase for main labels or consistent title case.

38. Use consistent font.
Why it matters: Mixed fonts reduce professionalism.
Application: Use one clean sans-serif font.

39. Use readable font size.
Why it matters: The user specifically requested slightly larger internal text.
Application: Avoid tiny text inside boxes.

40. Do not overload boxes with paragraphs.
Why it matters: A diagram is not a document.
Application: Move details into notes or companion checklist.

### Arrow Rules

41. Arrows must point in the true direction of work.
Why it matters: Incorrect arrows break process logic.
Application: Review every arrow before finalizing.

42. Arrows should connect box edges cleanly.
Why it matters: Arrows going too far into boxes look wrong.
Application: Start at the right edge and end at the left edge.

43. Arrowheads should stop before or at the receiving edge.
Why it matters: Arrows entering the box look messy.
Application: Do not push arrowheads into text.

44. Do not leave unfinished lines.
Why it matters: A loose line implies missing logic.
Application: Every line must connect or be removed.

45. Do not use decorative arrows.
Why it matters: Arrows are functional.
Application: Every arrow must represent dependency or sequence.

46. Use one arrow style throughout.
Why it matters: Consistency creates trust.
Application: Same line width and arrowhead.

47. Use branch arrows only when there is a real branch.
Why it matters: Fake branches create fake complexity.
Application: Only branch for parallel work or alternatives.

48. Use merge points when branches rejoin.
Why it matters: The reader must know when paths converge.
Application: Connect branches into a shared downstream box.

49. Do not route arrows through unrelated boxes.
Why it matters: This implies false dependency.
Application: Route around boxes cleanly.

50. Avoid arrows that reverse direction.
Why it matters: They disrupt flow.
Application: If feedback loops are needed, create a separate loop diagram.

### Critical Path

51. Every process diagram should identify the critical path.
Why it matters: Critical path determines launch timing.
Application: List it in notes and show it visually.

52. The critical path should be visually central.
Why it matters: The eye should follow the main work.
Application: Place it in the main horizontal row.

53. Support work should sit above or below the critical path.
Why it matters: It prevents false priority.
Application: Use secondary rows.

54. Do not force every task onto the critical path.
Why it matters: That creates an unrealistic plan.
Application: Separate must-do from nice-to-have.

55. Critical path tasks should be dependency-based.
Why it matters: A critical path is not a preference list.
Application: Ask: "Does this block launch?"

56. Critical path should end with a measurable outcome.
Why it matters: The path needs a finish line.
Application: Example: GO LIVE, FIRST CLIENT, SYSTEM OPERATIONAL.

57. Critical path should be reviewed after every major business change.
Why it matters: Strategy changes can change dependencies.
Application: Re-audit diagrams after new insights.

58. If a task can happen later, it should not appear as a launch blocker.
Why it matters: Avoids unnecessary delays.
Application: Put it in float.

59. If a task blocks revenue, it belongs near the critical path.
Why it matters: Revenue blockers are high priority.
Application: Sales, checkout, lead capture, response systems.

60. If a task blocks trust, it may belong near the critical path.
Why it matters: Trust affects conversion.
Application: Authority assets may be critical for launch.

### Float

61. Every major diagram should identify float.
Why it matters: Float shows schedule flexibility.
Application: Add FLOAT section.

62. Float tasks can move without delaying launch.
Why it matters: Prevents over-prioritization.
Application: Secondary blog posts, extra social posts, optional visuals.

63. Float should not be ignored.
Why it matters: Float work still creates value.
Application: Schedule it but do not let it block launch.

64. Float can become critical if strategy changes.
Why it matters: A new requirement can change priority.
Application: Reclassify when needed.

65. Content production often has partial float.
Why it matters: Some videos are critical, extras are optional.
Application: Homepage overview video may be critical; extra posts may float.

66. SEO often has partial float.
Why it matters: Technical SEO may be critical; long blog library can float.
Application: Separate technical SEO from content expansion.

67. Social media setup may be critical; daily posting backlog may float.
Why it matters: Presence is needed, volume can scale later.
Application: Create accounts before launch; backlog can grow.

68. Extra diagrams may float; core diagrams may be critical.
Why it matters: Not every diagram is needed for launch.
Application: Prioritize master, automation, funnel, sales diagrams.

69. Float should be documented, not hidden.
Why it matters: Hidden float causes confusion.
Application: Put it under the diagram.

70. Float should never be used as an excuse to skip quality.
Why it matters: Later work still needs standards.
Application: Maintain diagram quality for all tasks.

### Labeling And Numbering

71. Use diagram numbers consistently.
Why it matters: A manual needs references.
Application: 0.1, 1.1, 2.1, etc.

72. Use parent-child numbering.
Why it matters: Shows hierarchy.
Application: 3.1 Lead Acquisition, 3.2 Lead Database, 3.3 Outreach.

73. Use odd/even or sequential box numbering consistently.
Why it matters: A consistent pattern improves scanability.
Application: Do not randomly number boxes.

74. Box numbers should not collide with diagram numbers.
Why it matters: Avoids reference confusion.
Application: Diagram 3.1 can contain box [1], [3], [5].

75. Use clear diagram titles.
Why it matters: The reader should know the function instantly.
Application: "CLIENTSURGE AUTHORITY ENGINE" not "Content Stuff."

76. Use system names consistently across diagrams.
Why it matters: Terminology must match the business model.
Application: Use "Mission Control," not "Dashboard" in one diagram and "Control Center" in another unless defined.

77. Use the same term for the same system everywhere.
Why it matters: AI workers need consistent references.
Application: Choose "Free Automation Audit" and use it consistently.

78. Avoid unexplained acronyms.
Why it matters: New readers need clarity.
Application: Define GA4, GSC, GTM if used.

79. Label decision gates clearly.
Why it matters: Gate logic needs pass/fail clarity.
Application: "GO / NO-GO REVIEW" or "OWNER APPROVAL."

80. Label proof steps clearly.
Why it matters: Proof prevents fake completion.
Application: "UPLOAD PROOF" or "VERIFY SCREENSHOT."

### Hierarchy

81. Start with a master diagram.
Why it matters: Every system needs a parent.
Application: Diagram 0.1 ClientSurge Global AI Operating System.

82. Master diagrams should show divisions, not tiny tasks.
Why it matters: Too much detail ruins the master view.
Application: Use high-level systems.

83. Sub-diagrams should expand one parent box.
Why it matters: Keeps hierarchy clean.
Application: 2.1 Mission Control expands Mission Control from 0.1.

84. Do not mix multiple hierarchy levels in one diagram.
Why it matters: It creates clutter.
Application: Separate master plans from install checklists.

85. Each box in a master diagram can become a sub-diagram.
Why it matters: This creates a scalable manual.
Application: Lead Acquisition -> Lead Database -> Outreach -> Scoring.

86. Use consistent division names.
Why it matters: The manual should feel like one system.
Application: Foundation, Mission Control, Lead Acquisition, Funnel Engine, Authority Engine, Sales Engine, Delivery Engine, Analytics, Security, Platform Expansion.

87. Keep the master diagram strategic.
Why it matters: It should orient the business.
Application: Do not include tiny implementation tasks in 0.1.

88. Keep sub-diagrams operational.
Why it matters: They should guide execution.
Application: Show steps, gates, proof, owners.

89. Use process diagrams for workflows.
Why it matters: Not all diagrams are system maps.
Application: Sales process, lead flow, delivery flow.

90. Use system diagrams for architecture.
Why it matters: Architecture shows relationships.
Application: Software stack, data flow, dashboard modules.

### Accuracy

91. The diagram must reflect the real business model.
Why it matters: A pretty but inaccurate diagram is dangerous.
Application: ClientSurge is lead acquisition, not basic web design.

92. Do not omit major business divisions.
Why it matters: Missing systems create blind spots.
Application: Authority Engine must be included.

93. Do not overstate readiness.
Why it matters: Fake completion causes launch failure.
Application: Use accurate current status.

94. Do not make unverified claims.
Why it matters: Trust matters.
Application: Mark unverified items.

95. Do not assume a system exists if it is only planned.
Why it matters: Planning and execution differ.
Application: Label as "Planned" or "Placeholder."

96. Do not treat content as optional if it is needed for trust.
Why it matters: For ClientSurge, videos/diagrams/PDFs improve conversion.
Application: Authority assets belong in the plan.

97. Do not treat lead generation as one small box.
Why it matters: It is a full division.
Application: Break into SEO, outreach, paid ads, social, referrals.

98. Do not treat the website as the whole business.
Why it matters: The website is only the capture layer.
Application: Show automations, sales, delivery, analytics.

99. Do not hide sales steps.
Why it matters: Revenue requires proposal, contract, invoice, payment.
Application: Include them explicitly.

100. Do not ignore security and QA.
Why it matters: Launch failure damages trust.
Application: Security + QA must be a division.

### Visual Quality

101. Use clean black-and-white styling.
Why it matters: Matches the reference style.
Application: White boxes, black text, black borders.

102. Use minimal decorative elements.
Why it matters: Operational clarity matters more than art.
Application: Avoid icons unless carefully used.

103. Use enough white space.
Why it matters: Crowding makes diagrams hard to read.
Application: Increase canvas size if needed.

104. Use consistent margins.
Why it matters: Margins make diagrams feel professional.
Application: Outer border and internal spacing.

105. Keep note sections aligned.
Why it matters: Notes should feel part of the diagram.
Application: Place Current Position, Critical Path, Float below or beside main plan.

106. Use strong title hierarchy.
Why it matters: The eye needs order.
Application: System name, diagram number, objective.

107. Avoid tiny footnotes.
Why it matters: Printed diagrams need readability.
Application: Use readable note size.

108. Keep visual weight balanced.
Why it matters: A diagram should not feel lopsided.
Application: Distribute boxes evenly.

109. Use branches sparingly.
Why it matters: Too many branches become spaghetti.
Application: Split into sub-diagrams if complex.

110. If a diagram feels crowded, create another diagram.
Why it matters: A diagram should simplify, not overwhelm.
Application: Promote sections into sub-diagrams.

### Common Mistakes

111. Mistake: Using curved arrows.
Fix: Use straight arrows only.

112. Mistake: Different box sizes for similar tasks.
Fix: Standardize container size.

113. Mistake: Arrows going inside boxes.
Fix: Stop arrows at the edge.

114. Mistake: Branches with no merge point.
Fix: Show where parallel paths reconnect.

115. Mistake: Missing objective.
Fix: Add objective line.

116. Mistake: No critical path.
Fix: Identify and display the critical path.

117. Mistake: No float.
Fix: List flexible tasks.

118. Mistake: Too much text inside boxes.
Fix: Move details to checklist.

119. Mistake: Diagram looks good but business logic is wrong.
Fix: Audit against real workflow.

120. Mistake: No status or current position.
Fix: Add current position and completion status.

121. Mistake: Diagram does not connect to the master plan.
Fix: Reference the parent diagram and division.

## 6. Diagram Creation Workflow

1. Identify parent division.
2. Define objective.
3. List all steps.
4. Separate critical path from float.
5. Choose layout.
6. Create same-size boxes.
7. Add numbering.
8. Add arrows.
9. Add status notes.
10. Audit against checklist.
11. Revise.
12. Render/export if needed.

## 7. Diagram Audit Checklist

- Does the diagram have a title, diagram number, diagram type, and objective?
- Is there a clear START box?
- Does every box represent one task, system, gate, or deliverable?
- Are boxes same-size wherever possible?
- Are containers white with black outlines?
- Are all arrows straight?
- Do arrowheads stop at box edges without entering text?
- Does every line connect to a real source and destination?
- Is the critical path central and dependency-based?
- Is float explicitly listed?
- Are optional tasks kept off the critical path?
- Are labels short, operational, and consistent with ClientSurge terminology?
- Are proof, gate, or owner-approval steps labeled when relevant?
- Does the diagram avoid unverified completion claims?
- Does the diagram connect to the correct parent division?
- Is the output printable in black and white?
- Would a human or AI worker know the next action?

## 9. Common Mistakes And Immediate Fixes

- Mistake: Curved arrows.
  Immediate fix: Replace every curved connector with straight horizontal and vertical arrow segments.
- Mistake: Uneven boxes.
  Immediate fix: Standardize width and height for peer-level containers.
- Mistake: Arrows going into boxes.
  Immediate fix: Stop arrows at the receiving box edge and keep arrowheads out of text.
- Mistake: Missing objective.
  Immediate fix: Add an "Objective:" line under the title.
- Mistake: Missing START box.
  Immediate fix: Add a leftmost START container that identifies the trigger or first action.
- Mistake: Missing critical path.
  Immediate fix: Identify launch-blocking dependency steps and place them in the central row.
- Mistake: Missing float.
  Immediate fix: Add a FLOAT note listing flexible or non-blocking tasks.
- Mistake: Fake dependency.
  Immediate fix: Remove the arrow or move the task to a support row.
- Mistake: Fake completion.
  Immediate fix: Mark status as planned, pending proof, owner approval, blocked, or verified.
- Mistake: Too much text inside boxes.
  Immediate fix: Shorten box labels and move detail to notes or a companion checklist.
- Mistake: Diagram mixes hierarchy levels.
  Immediate fix: Split master strategy, operating system, and detailed checklist into separate diagrams.
- Mistake: Diagram ignores ClientSurge architecture.
  Immediate fix: Map it back to website capture, automations, voice AI, funnels, authority, dashboard, analytics, QA, sales, delivery, and revenue.
